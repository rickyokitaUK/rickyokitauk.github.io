DollCaptor

A 2.5D arena battler for up to four players: fight each other and capture fluffy monsters before the three-minute timer runs out.

Overview

DollCaptor began in March 2022 as a playable answer to a simple question: how much competitive fun fits into a three-minute round? Players move anime-style 2D sprites through a 3D arena, fight monsters and each other, pick up dessert-themed power-ups and race to capture the most monsters before time is up. The prototype has grown from a sprite-movement test into round flow, combat states, enemy spawning, menus and a new 3D level, with ChatGPT used along the way to speed up pre-production.

Design

A three-minute race to capture the most monsters

The game design document keeps the rules deliberately small, so every round is fast and readable.

  • ●Players: up to four, each with their own character, melee attack and spells.
  • ●Win condition: the player who has captured the most monsters when the three-minute timer ends.
  • ●Fainting: at zero HP a player faints for ten seconds and comes back with part of their health, which costs them capture time rather than knocking them out.
  • ●Monsters: fluffy creatures spawn on a timer around the arena and try to escape once their health runs low.
  • ●Items: dessert-themed pickups restore health, mana and stamina or temporarily boost attack and speed.

Core loop

One round

  1. 01

    Get ready

    Every character is frozen until the round starts

  2. 02

    Hunt

    Monsters spawn on a timer; rival players get in the way

  3. 03

    Fight

    Melee, spells, dash, jump and guard

    Monster down — it can be captured and counts toward the score
    HP reaches 0 — faint for 10 seconds and lose capture time
  4. 04

    Power up

    Dessert items restore HP, mana or stamina

  5. 05

    Time up

    Most captures wins

↺Repeat until the three-minute timer runs out

Engineering notes

Making 2D sprites work in a 3D arena

DollCaptor is 2.5D: hand-drawn sprites live in a 3D world with physics, NavMesh and real lighting. Most of the engineering is about keeping those two worlds consistent.

  • ●Eight-direction animation: a 2D Freeform Cartesian blend tree driven by MoveX and MoveY, which also remembers the last move direction so idle and attack animations face the right way.
  • ●NavMesh enemies: agents run with an angular speed of zero, so camera-facing sprites never rotate away from the player’s view.
  • ●Click-to-move: a ground-only raycast, reusing the mouse-picking approach from my 2007 final-year project.
  • ●Input versus UI: clicking Resume in the pause menu used to walk the character to the button. Pointer input is now ignored unless the game state is Playing.
  • ●Rendering: URP post-processing (bloom, tonemapping, vignette) on the main camera, with a separate UI camera so the HUD stays crisp; Shader Graph trails for spells and dashes.
  • ●Performance: projectiles with long trails prompted an object-pooling pass instead of instantiating and destroying them every cast.

Production log

From sprite test to playable rounds

A dated development log has tracked the project since day one. Selected milestones:

  • ●March 2022: project set up on URP, camera follow, sprite movement and the first spawned enemies.
  • ●April–June 2022: health bars, main menu, character select, NavMesh enemies, power-ups and a loading scene.
  • ●July–August 2022: eight-direction blend tree, attack animations with animation events, and the faint state.
  • ●January–February 2023: ChatGPT used to draft the GDD and concept prompts, and first versions of the power-up spawner and enemy AI.
  • ●April–May 2023: click-to-move, pause menu, UI camera, spell projectiles with trails, object pooling, mana attacks, jump and dash.
  • ●August–September 2024: a three-person team, the new Input System (keyboard and gamepad), a new 3D level with a skydome, and sword melee with hit effects.

AI-assisted pre-production

What ChatGPT was good for, and what it wasn’t

In early 2023 I used ChatGPT to draft the GDD outline, brainstorm titles and write character and monster bios, and an image generator for character concepts. It was quick at breadth: twenty-one title ideas, eight playable characters with weapons and abilities, and dozens of monster names.

The limits showed just as quickly. Many monster descriptions repeat the same “round body and short, stubby legs”, and generic sections such as budget and team structure did not describe a small independent project. The generated power-up spawner had a randomisation bug that only showed up in play. The rules that matter (three-minute rounds, the faint penalty, capture scoring) were set and tested in the prototype, not in the document.

What I built

  • ●Built the 2.5D character controller: eight-direction sprite animation, keyboard and click-to-move control, and gamepad support through the new Input System
  • ●Implemented combat states: melee attacks timed by animation events, mana-cast projectiles with stylised trails, dash, jump, and faint with a recovery timer
  • ●Created the round flow: Get Ready, timed play, Time Up, pause menu, loading scene, character select and the HP, mana and score HUD
  • ●Added timed monster spawning with NavMesh agents, monster health, capture counting and a power-up spawner

Key challenges

  • ●Keeping camera-facing sprites readable on NavMesh agents and while the camera pans
  • ●Stopping UI clicks, such as Resume in the pause menu, from also moving the character
  • ●Keeping trail-heavy projectiles and effects within budget

Next steps

  • ●Finish the capture mechanic and the captured-doll inventory
  • ●Give monsters fuller AI states, including a larger boss monster
  • ●Add mobile virtual controls, then online multiplayer as phase two
